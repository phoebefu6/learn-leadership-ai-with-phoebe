Leadership in the Age of AI · Session 4 of 6

The role-redesign bench

Sessions 1 to 3 built the argument: a role is a bundle of tasks, a freed hour has four places to go, and the junior rung is where cuts land first. Tonight the argument becomes arithmetic. The bench holds 34 occupations a professional-services firm employs, with all 624 of their O*NET 31.0 task statements and the published exposure label on each, and computes in your browser what a role's capacity really does as you add the things a naive case leaves out. You set the assumptions; the bench shows what they cost, prints the number a headcount cut on saved hours would get wrong, and lets you break it on purpose to see how rosy a case gets when review is free.

Part 0

Where we start

Three kinds of number sit on the bench, and each one carries its tag. Real: the task statements, importance and frequency ratings of 34 occupations from the O*NET 31.0 Database, and the task-level exposure labels released with Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin and Rock's 2023 study, both read from the published files. Constructed: Marlowe Group, the invented 4,000-person firm, with 3,660 of its people spread across those 34 roles and a junior grade in each. Modelled: every speed-up, review, demand and cost figure, each one an assumption with a default taken from the source map and a slider you can move.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A five-rung ladder that turns a role's task list into a capacity range a board can question, the size of the gap between the naive exposed share and that range for Marlowe's largest role and for the whole firm, the decision the anti-lever gets wrong and by how much, and a habit of asking any AI business case what happens to its number when review time goes back in.

Part 1 · covers what the bench computes

How a role becomes a number

7 min live

The bench runs the same eight steps for every role, in the browser and in an independent Python reference run that checked every number on this page. Two steps are real data, one is a proxy, and the rest are assumptions you can see and move. The ladder rungs add the steps one at a time, so you can watch which step moves the number most.

LiveThe five rungs, in one sentence each3 min▶

Rung 1, naive: the share of the role's tasks labelled E1 or E2, read as the share of the job freed. It is the number a slide says when it says "82 percent of this role is exposed". Rung 2: each task weighted by its share of the week, and only the assumed speed-up saved on exposed tasks. Rung 3: review and verification time added back on every exposed task, because someone has to check the output. Rung 4: part of what is freed absorbed by new or rebounding demand. Rung 5: rung 4 at a slow case and a fast case, with the reskilling payback as a range, which is the only form a board should accept.

The reskill band covers training time and programme cost for everyone in the role, expressed as a share of one person's loaded year. Payback is the band divided by the freed FTE per year, in months. If the slow case frees nothing, the bench says "never" rather than inventing a number.

Build-along · the bench

34 real roles, five rungs, one anti-lever

★ 26 min · the main event

The bench opens on Marlowe's management analysts, the eleven tasks from session 1, on the human annotators' labels. Work down the rungs in order and say out loud which step moved the number before you click the next one. Then pick the role you inventoried in session 1, or the closest one in the list, and paste your hours into the time-share column.

Read it in this order. The verdict line first, then the tag on each number, then the ladder bars. For the management analysts on the defaults, the naive rung says 81.8% of the role is freed, 736.4 FTE of 900. Rung 2 says 11.8%. Review takes it to 3.9%, and demand rebound to 2.9%, which is 26.1 FTE. The board range on rung 5 runs from -48.0 to 177.4 FTE. Your numbers will differ as soon as you move an assumption, which is the point.

Part 2 · covers what the ladder shows across Marlowe

The naive case is not a little high, it is a different number

6 min live

Run the ladder for all 34 roles at once and add up the FTE-equivalents across Marlowe's 3,660 people in the sample. These are the numbers the bench prints on its defaults, written down so they can be argued with; an independent Python run agrees with the browser engine on every value.

LiveFinding 1: counting tasks is not counting time2 min▶

Across the 34 roles the naive rung frees 3,034.7 FTE of 3,660. Weighting tasks by time and saving only the assumed speed-up gives 474.7. The biggest single drop on the whole ladder is this one, and it needs no pessimism at all: a task labelled exposed is a task that could take less time, not a task that takes no time. Most of the gap is that difference.

LiveFinding 2: review eats the software-dependent tasks2 min▶

Adding back 10 percent review time on every exposed task takes the firm from 474.7 to 171.5 FTE. The reason is the mix. In these roles most exposed tasks are E2, needing software on top; on the default an E2 task saves 12.5 percent of its time, and checking its output costs 10. An E1 task saves 25 and keeps 15. For the management analysts, seven of the nine exposed tasks are E2, which is why their rung 3 sits at 3.9%. A business case that leaves review out is mostly counting E2 savings that review would take back.

Self-studyFinding 3: switch the labeller and the firm changes2 min▶

Switch the exposure labels to the GPT-4 classifier. The naive rung for the firm moves from 3,034.7 to 3,285.9 FTE and rung 4 from 128.7 to 135.5. Across the 34 roles, 108 of 624 tasks flip between exposed and not exposed with the rater. Neither label set is ground truth, as the study's authors say; a board paper names which one it used, and the bench lets you show both.

Real world

What one field study measured, and what it did not. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond followed 5,179 customer-support agents: issues resolved per hour rose 14 percent on average, 34 percent for novice and low-skilled agents, with minimal effect for the most experienced. That is a throughput gain on one task in one firm, measured. It is the slow end of the bench's E1 range, not its default, because a support chat is closer to the tool's sweet spot than most professional-services tasks are, and because nothing in it says what the firm did with the hours.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever, the break button and the board range

The cut that leaves the checking unstaffed

6 min live

The anti-lever is the most common decision in the room: take the hours a tool saves, divide by a working year, and cut that many people. The bench takes rung 2's hours, cuts that headcount from the junior grade first, and then prints what the cut leaves behind.

LiveThe anti-lever: count hours saved as headcount cut2 min▶

For the management analysts on the defaults, rung 2 frees 106.6 FTE of time, so the anti-lever cuts 106 people. Rung 4, with review and absorbed demand back in, frees 26.1. The cut leaves 79.9 FTE of work with nobody to do it: 71.8 FTE of checking AI output that no one is staffed for, and 8.7 FTE of demand that arrived anyway. Taken from the junior grade first, the cut takes it from 405 to 299. Across the firm the same rule cuts 453 people and leaves 324.3 FTE unstaffed.

The honest nuance: the shortfall is only as large as the review and demand assumptions make it. Set review to zero and demand to zero and the gap almost closes, which is exactly the case the anti-lever is silently assuming. The junior-first order is a model, chosen because exposed tasks skew to entry-level work; session 3 covers why that is the rung a firm can least afford to lose.

LiveThe break button: drop the review time2 min▶

A bench that can only produce the answer you hoped for measures nothing. The break button sets review time to zero and recomputes. For the analysts, rung 4 jumps from 26.1 to 80.0 FTE; for the firm, from 128.7 to 356.0. Nothing about the work changed. The case got rosier because it now treats checking AI output as free.

That is not a hypothetical worry. In the METR trial, experienced developers forecast that AI would cut their completion time by 24 percent and believed afterwards that it had cut it by 20; measured, it made them 19 percent slower. Review and rework are where that gap lives.

Self-studyReading the board range2 min▶

Rung 5 recomputes rung 4 at a slow case (14 percent saved on E1 tasks, 15 percent review, half the freed time absorbed) and a fast case (50 percent saved, 5 percent review, nothing absorbed). For the analysts that is -48.0 to 177.4 FTE, and a reskilling payback from 2 months to never. A slow case below zero means review costs more than the tool saves; the bench prints "never" for its payback rather than a number.

A range this wide is the finding, not a failure of the model. It says the decision turns on two numbers nobody has measured for Marlowe: how long checking takes, and how much new work arrives. Session 5 turns that into a choice between building, buying, reskilling and waiting until one of them is measured.

Part 4 · covers how big the speed-up really is

The speed-up, as the studies disagree

5 min live

Every business case rests on one number: how much faster a task gets. The studies do not agree on it, and they are not measuring the same thing. Laid on one line for orientation, with the bench's range marked, they make the case for a range rather than a point.

LiveThe disagreement, as it stands3 min▶

Field gains are real and uneven. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond measured 14 percent more issues resolved per hour, concentrated among novices. Dell'Acqua and colleagues' 758 consultants completed 12.2 percent more subtasks and worked roughly 25 percent faster on tasks inside the tool's range; on a task built to fall outside it, 84.5 percent were correct without AI and 70.6 percent with it (reported, from Harvard Business School's summary of the published paper).

Experienced people on their own work can get slower. The METR trial of 16 experienced developers on 246 tasks measured a 19 percent slowdown, against their own forecast of a 24 percent speed-up.

The economy-wide view is small. Acemoglu's estimate is no more than a 0.66 percent rise in total factor productivity over ten years, less than 0.53 percent once hard-to-learn tasks are counted. That is a different unit, a whole economy over a decade, and it is the low side of a live argument, not a refutation of the field studies. None of this is settled, and the bench does not settle it: it makes you choose an assumption and shows you what the choice costs.

The sentence to use with a board. "On the published task data and our stated assumptions, this role ends up anywhere from 48.0 FTE short, if checking costs more than the tool saves, to 177.4 FTE freed; the range is wide because we have not yet measured our own review time or our demand, and the first quarter of the programme measures both."

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · For the management analysts, rung 1 says 81.8% and rung 2 says 11.8%. What explains most of that drop?

Review arrives at rung 3 and demand at rung 4, and both rungs use the human labels by default. The drop from rung 1 to rung 2 is the change of unit from tasks to time: an exposed task gets faster, it does not vanish.

2 · The anti-lever cuts 106 analysts on rung 2's hours. What does the bench say that decision gets wrong?

The cut is sized on time saved before anyone checks the output or any new work arrives. Both still happen. The shortfall is modelled, and its size moves with the review and demand assumptions; its sign does not, unless both are zero.

3 · A vendor's case shows a much larger saving than your rung 4. Which question does the break button teach you to ask first?

Dropping review makes the case rosier with no change in the work: for the analysts, rung 4 goes from 26.1 to 80.0 FTE. A count of exposed tasks is rung 1's mistake, and a model version says nothing about who checks the output.
