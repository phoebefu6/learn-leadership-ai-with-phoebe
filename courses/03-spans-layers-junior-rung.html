New shapes: spans, layers and the missing junior rung

Where we start

Marlowe Group is constructed: an invented professional-services firm of 4,000 people whose board wants a workforce plan for AI. Part 1 · covers org-level layers and ratios

The firm as layers and ratios

5 min live

A workforce plan written role by role hides the thing a board most needs to see: how the roles stack. Every client question at Marlowe (constructed) passes from a partner to an engagement manager to a senior analyst to a junior analyst and back. The number of layers sets how long that round trip takes; the ratios between layers set who is coordinating whom and how many juniors each senior is growing into the next senior.

LiveThree ratios, read at firm level3 min▶

Layers is how many hand-offs sit between the client and the person doing the first draft. People per coordinating manager is how much of the middle exists to route, compile and chase. Juniors per senior is the pipeline: how many people are being grown, right now, into the seniors the firm will need in five years. At Marlowe (constructed) the three read 4, 15 and about 0.8.

AI moves all three at once and in different directions. If assistants take the compiling, the second ratio can rise without anyone feeling overloaded. If assistants take first drafts, the third ratio can fall without anyone deciding it should. A plan that reports only total headcount will show neither. How many people one manager can lead well inside a single team is a different question, taught in learn-team-management ↗; here the unit is the firm.

Self-studyThe span rules that have no source2 min▶

"The right span of control is 5 to 7" and "Dunbar's number" turn up in almost every org-design deck. As rules for how a whole firm should be layered, no primary source sets either one. Treat them as folklore: a number that sounds measured and is not.

The professional norm is to set a ratio from the work, not from a rule of thumb. A manager whose week is mostly compiling status can carry more people once an assistant compiles; a manager whose week is mostly coaching on live client work cannot. Write down which kind of week each layer has before you change its ratio, and say where the number came from when you present it.

Real world

A familiar pattern: the ratio nobody owns. Total headcount has an owner in finance. Spans have an owner in each business line. The junior-to-senior ratio usually has no owner at all: graduate intake is set by one team, promotions by another, and the ratio between them drifts. When a firm slows graduate hiring because first drafts got faster, the drift has a direction for the first time, and it shows up years later as a shortage of experienced seniors that no single decision caused.

Part 2 · covers what assistants do to the coordination layer

Thinning the middle, and what rides along with it

5 min live

The case for a flatter firm is simple to state. Much of a middle manager's week is coordination: compiling status, summarising for the layer above, routing requests, chasing hand-offs. Those are the tasks an assistant reaches first. Take them out and the layer can carry more people, or carry fewer managers. The case against is quieter: some of that coordination was also where juniors learned.

LiveThe flattening case, and what it leaves out3 min▶

Marlowe's chief operating officer (constructed) proposes taking 20 of the 60 engagement managers out and spreading their analysts across the other 40. On the coordination rows alone the arithmetic works: an assistant compiles the status, drafts the partner summary and tracks the hand-offs. About twenty-two analysts per manager instead of fifteen looks like the same work done by fewer people.

What it leaves out is the second half of the manager's week. Coaching analysts on live work does not get faster because the status report did; it gets harder with more analysts to coach. And part of what looked like coordination was teaching: the weekly status meeting was where a junior heard a senior frame the client's problem out loud. The professional norm is to list both halves of the layer's work before changing its ratio, and to name who will do the development work the layer used to do as a side effect.

Self-studyReading a prediction as a prediction2 min▶

Gartner's "Top Strategic Predictions for 2025 and Beyond" (October 2024) is reported here from press coverage, because Gartner's own page could not be read. It predicts that through 2026, 20 percent of organisations will use AI to flatten their structure, eliminating more than half of current middle-management positions. The same note flags the cost: disrupted mentoring paths and fewer development opportunities for junior employees.

A prediction from an analyst firm is a forecast of what organisations will do, not evidence that doing it works. Quote it as "reported" and as a prediction. It is useful to a board for a narrower reason: the forecaster who expects flattening also names the junior pipeline as its cost, so the two belong on the same page of any plan.

Real world

Flattening and the mentoring path, in one forecast. Gartner's 2024 predictions note (reported) puts the two side by side: 20 percent of organisations using AI to flatten structure through 2026, eliminating more than half of current middle-management positions, and in the same breath disrupted mentoring paths and fewer development opportunities for junior employees. No published study yet measures what flattening did to junior development at firm level; treat the cost as named, not sized.

Part 3 · covers the junior rung as the pipeline to senior

The bottom rung is the pipeline

5 min live

At Marlowe (constructed), the exposed analyst tasks are mostly junior work: gathering information, first analysis, first drafts of findings. Session 4's bench makes the consequence exact. If the firm cuts headcount on the hours the tools save, the cut lands on the junior grade first. The evidence on what AI does to juniors points two ways, and a board should hear both before it decides.

LiveTwo readings, both cited, neither settled4 min▶

Reading one. Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied 5,179 customer-support agents given an AI assistant. Issues resolved per hour rose 14 percent on average, by 34 percent for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal impact on experienced and highly skilled ones. Read for a board: the tool narrows the gap between a new hire and an experienced one, so a junior with an assistant may be worth more, sooner, than a junior without.

Reading two. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen, in the August 2026 revision of "Canaries in the Coal Mine?", use ADP payroll data through June 2026. Employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is 19 percent below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, with no comparable gap for experienced workers, and the declines concentrate where AI mainly substitutes for work rather than complements it. The authors call these facts descriptive, not causal: some trends predate generative AI, the gap weakens when education is controlled for, and they find no evidence of widespread economy-wide displacement.

The two do not contradict each other so much as measure different things: one, what a junior produces once hired; the other, how many juniors are hired at all. A board paper should carry both, with their limits, and should not claim either one settles what the rung is worth at your firm.

LiveWhy a cut on hours lands on the junior grade2 min▶

The bench's anti-lever is a stated model: exposed tasks skew to entry-level work, which is consistent with both studies above, so a cut "on the hours" is taken from the junior grade first. On its default assumptions, for Marlowe's 900 management analysts (constructed headcount), the cut is 106 people, the junior grade falls from 405 to 299, and 79.9 FTE of work is left unstaffed: 71.8 FTE of review the cut forgot and 8.7 FTE of demand that came back. Across the firm's 34 roles the same move cuts 453 people and leaves 324.3 FTE unstaffed.

The junior loss does not show in year one. It shows when the firm needs seniors and has a thinner layer to promote from. Session 4 lets you run the anti-lever on your own role; session 6 turns junior intake into a tripwire the board watches.

Real world

The canaries, in their authors' own limits. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, revised August 2026) find employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations 19 percent below trend, and no comparable gap for experienced workers. They also say what the finding is not: descriptive rather than causal, partly predating generative AI, weaker once education is controlled for, and not evidence of widespread displacement. Quote the 19 percent with the revision date; the first 2025 version gave a different figure.

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A director proposes a span of 5 to 7 for every manager in the firm, "because that is the right span". What is the sound response?

"5 to 7" and Dunbar's number are folklore as org-wide rules: neither has a primary source setting how a firm should be layered. A manager whose week is compiling status can carry more people once an assistant compiles; one whose week is coaching on live work cannot.

2 · The board hears that novices gain most from AI assistants and also that early-career employment in exposed occupations is 19 percent below trend. What should the paper say?

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond measured issues resolved per hour among agents already employed; Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen measured employment of 22 to 25 year olds, and say their facts are descriptive, not causal. Disagreement between careful studies is information, not noise; it tells the board which number to watch.

3 · Why does a headcount cut on saved hours tend to empty the junior rung first?

The anti-lever is a stated model: exposed tasks skew to entry-level work, consistent with both junior studies, so a cut on the hours takes from the junior grade. It also leaves 79.9 FTE of review and returning demand unstaffed, and the pipeline loss shows years later, when the firm needs seniors.
