Leadership in the Age of AI · Session 1 of 6

The org chart is a bundle of tasks

A board asks what AI means for the headcount plan, and the first answer usually arrives as a list of job titles: these roles grow, those shrink. That answer skips a step. AI does not reach a job title; it reaches the tasks inside it, one at a time, and most jobs hold tasks it barely touches next to tasks it changes a lot. Tonight we take one role apart into the tasks the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database lists for it, label each task with the exposure rubric from the most cited study of the question, read what that label does and does not mean, and watch two careful labellers disagree about the same tasks.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Marlowe Group is an invented professional-services firm of 4,000 people: consultants, accountants, analysts, lawyers, technologists and the operations staff around them. Its board has asked the executive team for a workforce plan for AI, with one question underneath it: how many people will we need in three years, and in which roles? The chief executive, Hana Reyes, wants an answer she can defend line by line, not a percentage from a vendor deck. Marlowe, Hana and every Marlowe number are invented for teaching; the task data underneath them is real.

Part 1 · covers roles as task bundles and the O*NET task data

A title is a container, the tasks are the contents

5 min live

O*NET, the occupational database run for the U.S. Department of Labor, describes each occupation as a list of task statements, each rated by people in the job for how important it is and how often they do it. For a management analyst, Marlowe's largest role with 900 people (constructed headcount), O*NET 31.0 lists eleven tasks. Read them and the role stops being one thing that AI either takes or leaves.

LiveWhy a list of titles misleads a board3 min▶

"Analysts are exposed to AI" sounds like a finding. It is a summary of eleven different facts. Interviewing staff and watching how a unit actually works is untouched. Gathering and organising information is exposed, but only once someone builds or buys software around a language model. Planning a study of a work problem is exposed directly. A plan that cuts "analysts" by a percentage cuts all eleven tasks by that percentage, including the two that nothing has changed.

The fix is a change of unit. Marlowe's plan will list tasks, then add up what happens to them role by role, then decide what the roles should become. That is slower than a list of titles and it is the only version the board can interrogate: every line has a source, a label and an assumption someone can argue with.

Self-studyWhat O*NET is, and what it does not record3 min▶

O*NET 31.0 is the current release of the database, published by the U.S. Department of Labor's Employment and Training Administration under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence. For each occupation it holds task statements, each typed core or supplemental and rated for importance on a 1 to 5 scale and for frequency as a spread across seven categories, from "yearly or less" to "hourly or more".

What it does not hold is hours. Nothing in O*NET says how much of an analyst's week goes on gathering information versus interviewing staff. That gap matters in session 2, where a task count turns into a time share, and it is why the bench in session 4 lets you overwrite every time share with your own. O*NET describes U.S. occupations; a firm elsewhere uses it as a starting list and checks it against its own people.

Real world

The task, not the job, is how the field studies were designed. The best-known field evidence measured one task each: customer-support agents resolving chats (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 5,179 agents, 14 percent more issues resolved per hour) and consultants doing a set of tasks inside and outside what the tool handled well (Dell'Acqua and colleagues, 758 consultants). None of them measured a job. Session 2 reads their numbers; tonight the point is the unit. The labour economics behind it, displacement and reinstatement, is taught in Economics, session 7 ↗.

Part 2 · covers the E0, E1, E2 exposure rubric

Three labels, and what "exposed" means

5 min live

Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin and Rock's 2023 study, "GPTs are GPTs", labelled every O*NET task with one question: could a large language model cut the time this task takes by at least half, at a quality the recipient would not notice or mind? Their answer has three values, and the middle one is where most professional-services work sits.

LiveExposure is not automation, in the authors' own words3 min▶

The rubric asks about potential time saved, not about whether the task disappears. The authors say a high exposure share "does not necessarily suggest that their tasks can be fully automated", that technical feasibility does not guarantee productivity or automation outcomes, and that real-world time savings would likely be "slightly or significantly lower" than their estimates. They make no prediction about adoption timelines.

Their headline numbers are about potential: about 80 percent of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10 percent of their tasks affected, and about 19 percent could see at least half their tasks affected. Their human annotators put the share of workers with more than half their tasks exposed to an LLM alone, with no extra software, at 3 percent. The distance between 3 and 19 percent is the E2 box above: the software someone still has to build, buy and trust.

Self-studyAlpha, beta, zeta: three ways to add the labels up3 min▶

The study turns labels into an occupation score three ways. Alpha counts only E1 tasks. Beta counts E1 plus half of E2, the half standing for the extra investment the software needs. Zeta counts E1 plus all of E2, an upper bound. All three weight every task in an occupation equally; the authors report that weighting by O*NET importance did not change their results much.

For a leader the choice of measure is a choice of story. Zeta tells the board what could happen if every piece of software were built. Alpha tells it what a chat window does on Monday. Beta's half-weight on E2 is the bench's default in session 4: an E2 task saves half of what an E1 task saves, a stated assumption you can move.

Real world

The ILO reads the same kind of data as augmentation. The International Labour Organization's refined global index (Gmyrek and colleagues, Working Paper 140, 2025) puts one in four workers worldwide in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure, and 3.3 percent of global employment in the highest exposure category, with women more exposed than men (4.7 against 2.4 percent) and high-income countries far more than low-income ones (34 against 11 percent). Its 2023 predecessor concluded that the main effect is likely to augment rather than automate jobs. Exposure studies describe where change can land; they do not say what employers will do with it.

Part 3 · covers who produced the label

Two careful labellers, two answers

5 min live

Every exposure label was made by someone. Eloundou and colleagues used two sources on the same rubric: human annotators who knew what language models could do, and GPT-4 prompted with the rubric. They report human labels as their primary results and say plainly that neither should be treated as the definitive ground truth.

LiveWhy the labellers disagree3 min▶

The authors name the limits of both. Their annotators knew language models well but were "not occupationally diverse", so they were judging work in occupations they had not done. GPT-4's labels moved with the wording of the rubric, the order of the prompt and the examples given. The two agree on most tasks; where they disagree it is usually between neighbouring labels, E1 against E2, and sometimes across the line that matters most for a plan, exposed against not exposed.

For a board paper this means one habit: every exposure number carries the name of its rater. "Nine of eleven analyst tasks are exposed, on the human annotators' labels" is a sentence someone can check. "Analysts are 82 percent exposed" is not.

Self-studyThe better rater is your own people2 min▶

The study's own suggestion for better labels is workers in the occupation, or at least people with deep knowledge of its tasks. Inside a firm that is cheap to get: an hour with three experienced people in a role, going down the task list with the rubric, produces labels for your version of the job, which is rarely exactly the U.S. average one. The build-along below starts that conversation.

An occupation-level index exists too: Felten, Raj and Seamans' AI Occupational Exposure scores (Strategic Management Journal, 2021) give one number per occupation. It is useful for a first sort and useless for redesign, because redesign happens at the task.

Build-along

A task inventory for one role

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Hana asks each executive for one page per role before anyone talks about headcount. You will build that page for one role at your own organisation, or for Marlowe's management analysts if you prefer. It takes six columns, and the last one is the column O*NET cannot fill for you.

Pick one role with at least 50 people in it. Large roles are where a redesign moves the most capacity and where a mistake costs the most. Write the role, its headcount and where it sits in the structure.

★ Step 1 · the roleRole: Management analyst Headcount: 900 (Marlowe, constructed) Sits in: the advisory practice, under engagement managers Why this one: largest role at the firm; most client hours

Copy the task list from O*NET. Search the occupation on O*NET OnLine and take the task statements as written, with their importance and frequency if you want them. Mark any task your version of the role does not do, and add any it does that O*NET misses.

★ Step 2 · the tasksSource: O*NET 31.0, 13-1111.00 Management Analysts, 11 tasks Keep: all 11 Add: "Draft client proposals and statements of work" (not in O*NET's list; Marlowe analysts spend real time here) Note: added tasks have no O*NET rating and no published label

Label each task with the rubric, and write the rater. Use the published human label where one exists. For any task you added, label it yourself with the two questions from Part 2 and write your name as the rater.

★ Step 3 · labels and ratersGather and organise information ... E2 (Eloundou human) Interview staff, observe on site ... E0 (Eloundou human) Plan studies of work problems ... E1 (Eloundou human; GPT-4 says E2) Draft client proposals ... E1 (our label, 3 seniors)

Write who does each task today. Junior, senior, or both. This column is the one session 3 needs: if the exposed tasks are mostly junior work, the junior rung is where the redesign lands, and the junior rung is where next decade's seniors come from.

★ Step 4 · who does itGather and organise information mostly junior Analyse data, develop solutions junior drafts, senior decides Interview and observe on site senior Document findings, recommendations junior drafts, senior signs

Estimate hours per week for each task, as a range. O*NET has no hours, so this is your people's estimate. Ask three people in the role separately and write the spread, not an average. Session 2 turns these into time shares.

★ Step 5 · hours, as a rangeGather and organise information 8 to 12 h/wk Analyse data, develop solutions 6 to 10 h/wk Interview and observe on site 3 to 6 h/wk (constructed for Marlowe; your numbers will differ)

Write the one-line reading for the board. One sentence per role that names the rater, the label counts and the biggest unknown.

★ Step 6 · the board lineManagement analyst: 2 of 11 O*NET tasks directly exposed and 7 more exposed only through software we do not yet have (human labels, Eloundou et al.); the hours are our estimate and the largest unknown.

Keep the sheet; it is the input to the bench. Session 4 loads the same eleven tasks from O*NET, with the published labels, and lets you overwrite the time shares with the hours you wrote in step 5.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Nine of the eleven management-analyst tasks carry an exposure label. What can the board conclude?

A label is a statement about potential time saved on one task, not about automation or headcount; the authors say so. A and B turn a count of tasks into a share of work and then into people, two steps no data supports. D is wrong on the facts: the primary labels are human annotators'.

2 · What separates an E1 task from an E2 task?

The difference is what it takes to get the saving: a chat window, or software someone builds or buys. Nothing in the rubric says a task disappears. In the study's beta measure an E2 task counts half, not double, because of that extra investment.

3 · The human annotators and GPT-4 disagree on whether some tasks are exposed. What should a board paper do?

The authors say neither source is the definitive ground truth and that labels from people in the occupation would be better. Averaging hides the disagreement, dropping tasks hides the work, and GPT-4's labels moved with the wording of its prompt.
