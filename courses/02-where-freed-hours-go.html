Leadership in the Age of AI · Session 2 of 6

Where the freed hours actually go

Session 1 ended with eleven labelled tasks for one role. The board's next question is about hours: if those tasks get faster, how much time comes back, and where does it end up? The honest answer has four steps. A count of exposed tasks has to become a share of the week. The speed-up has to come from evidence, and the evidence disagrees with itself. Checking the tool's work is work, and it comes out of the saving first. And whatever is left still has four places to go, only one of which is a smaller team. Tonight we walk one freed hour through all four.

Part 0

Where we start

Marlowe Group is still constructed: an invented professional-services firm of 4,000 people whose chief executive, Hana Reyes, owes her board a workforce plan for AI. After session 1 she has a task inventory for the advisory practice's 900 management analysts, with a published exposure label and a rater on every task. A vendor deck on her desk reads that inventory one way: nine of eleven tasks exposed, so most of the work goes. Tonight is about why that reading is off by a factor of about seven before anyone has argued about the evidence, and what the evidence then does to the rest.

Part 1 · covers task counts against time shares

A count of tasks is not a share of the week

5 min live

"Nine of eleven tasks are exposed" treats every task as if it took the same time and as if exposure removed all of it. Neither is true. Weigh each task by how much of the week it takes, apply a saving that matches its label, and Marlowe's analysts (constructed headcount) go from 81.8 percent exposed by count to 11.8 percent of their time freed, on the bench's default assumptions.

LiveA count treats every task as a whole week3 min▶

A count makes two silent assumptions. The first is that every task takes the same share of time, so "planning a study" weighs as much as "gathering and organising information", which analysts do far more often. The second is that an exposed task is a gone task, when the rubric only says a language model could cut its time, and by "at least half" for E1 at best. Remove both assumptions and the analysts' 81.8 percent becomes 11.8 percent of their time freed on paper, or 106.6 of 900 FTE. That one correction is bigger than every argument about the evidence that follows.

The 11.8 percent uses the bench's defaults, which are assumptions: an E1 task saves 25 percent of its time and an E2 task half that, 12.5 percent, following the study's half-weight on E2. Move the E1 saving across its range, 14 to 50 percent, and the number moves with it. What does not move is the order of magnitude: a count overstates the freed time several times over.

Self-studyThe proxy, and how to replace it3 min▶

O*NET 31.0 rates each task for importance on a 1 to 5 scale and for frequency as a spread over seven categories, from "yearly or less" to "hourly or more". It records no hours. The bench's time share is therefore a proxy: importance times mean frequency, divided by the sum across the role's tasks. A task rated important and done often gets a large share; a rare one gets a small share. It is a reasonable first guess and it is not a measurement.

Eloundou and colleagues did not weight tasks by time at all: their exposure scores count every task equally, and they report that weighting by importance did not change their results much. That is fine for comparing occupations across an economy. It is not fine for a headcount plan, where a ten-minute weekly task and a twenty-hour one cannot count the same. The fix is the column from session 1: hours per task from three people in the role, as a range. Where you have those, they replace the proxy.

Real world

A familiar pattern: the use-case count. An AI business case lists the use cases found in each function, counts how many of a role's activities they touch, and multiplies that share by the role's headcount. The arithmetic is clean and the unit is wrong: it counts activities, not hours, and it assumes each touched activity disappears. Asking "what share of the week, and how much faster, on whose evidence" usually shrinks the headline before any assumption is challenged.

Part 2 · covers the speed-up evidence and its spread

The speed-up is a range, and the studies disagree

5 min live

How much faster does a task get? Four careful studies give four different answers, measured four different ways, on four different kinds of worker. They do not average into one number, and a plan that picks the most flattering one has picked a side in a live disagreement. The board needs the spread and the conditions behind each end of it.

LiveRead each number with its conditions3 min▶

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied 5,179 customer-support agents. An AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by 14 percent on average, by 34 percent for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal impact on the experienced and highly skilled. Dell'Acqua and colleagues gave 758 consultants tasks inside and outside what the tool handles well; inside, they completed 12.2 percent more subtasks, roughly 25 percent faster, with work rated about 32 percent higher quality (reported, from the published version's summary). Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein at METR watched 16 experienced developers do 246 tasks in their own repositories: they forecast AI would cut their time by 24 percent, believed afterwards it had cut it by 20 percent, and were measured 19 percent slower.

These are not contradictions to be averaged away. They are different workers on different work. The gains are largest for novices on well-structured, high-volume tasks; they shrink for experts; and for experts on their own complex work, one careful study found a loss the experts themselves did not notice. Marlowe's analysts (constructed) are a mix of all three, which is why the bench carries the E1 saving as a range, 14 to 50 percent, and not as a point.

LiveOutside the line, correctness falls2 min▶

Dell'Acqua and colleagues also built a brand-strategy task the tool would get wrong. Without AI, 84.5 percent of consultants got it right; with GPT-4, 70.6 percent; with GPT-4 and a prompt overview, 60 percent (reported). The tool made people faster and more confident on a task where it was wrong, and more guidance made it worse. Bottom-half performers improved 31 percent overall, top-half 11 percent.

For a plan this changes the arithmetic: the saving on a task is worth something only if someone catches the cases where the output is wrong, and that catching is work. Part 3 puts it on the books. If you meet the figures "40 percent higher quality" or "19 percentage points less likely to be correct", they are from the 2023 working paper; the published version's summary gives the numbers above. The full study is taught in AI consulting ↗.

Self-studyThe macro view is smaller still2 min▶

Acemoglu's "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI" (Economic Policy, 2025) adds up task-level savings across the U.S. economy and puts the total factor productivity gain over ten years at no more than 0.66 percent, and less than 0.53 percent once hard-to-learn tasks are counted. The gain depends on two things: the fraction of tasks affected and the average cost saving on each, the same two quantities this session separates. He also expects AI to widen the gap between capital and labour income.

Set that beside the field studies and the disagreement is plain. Task-level studies find double-digit gains on some work; a macro estimate built from the same kind of evidence finds a small total. Both can be right: large gains on a narrow slice of tasks add up to a small number across everything. A firm sits between the two, and only its own measurement says where.

Real world

The forecast was wrong in the hopeful direction. In METR's study the developers were experienced, the tools were early-2025 assistants, and the tasks were real issues in repositories they knew well. They predicted a 24 percent saving, felt a 20 percent saving afterwards, and the clock said 19 percent slower (Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein, arXiv 2507.09089). A survey of how much time people think AI saves them is a measure of belief. Plan on measured time.

Part 3 · covers review load, demand rebound and the four destinations

Four places a freed hour can go

5 min live

An hour the tool saves does not arrive as an empty hour. Some of it goes straight back into checking what the tool produced. Some is taken up by more work, because cheaper output invites more requests. What remains is a decision: leave it as slack, fill it with new tasks, or run a smaller team. Only the last one shows up in a headcount plan, and on Marlowe's analysts (constructed) it is the smallest share.

LiveReview comes out first3 min▶

Every exposed task now has a new step: someone reads what the tool produced and decides whether it is right. The bench assumes that step costs 10 percent of the task's original time, with a range of 5 to 15 percent, and says plainly that it is an assumption: no study measures review time at firm scale. The two studies that come closest point in the same direction. METR's developers were measured slower with AI, which shows checking can cost more than the tool saves; Dell'Acqua and colleagues found unchecked output outside the tool's range was wrong more often.

On the defaults the effect is largest on E2 tasks, which are most of the analysts' exposed work. An E2 task saves 12.5 percent of its time and costs 10 percent to check, so its net saving is 2.5 percent. That is why review takes 71.8 of the analysts' 106.6 freed FTE and leaves 34.9, or 3.9 percent of the role. If review is slower than assumed, the saving can go negative, and the bench's slow case does exactly that.

LiveDemand rebound: the number nobody has measured3 min▶

When analysis gets cheaper, clients and internal teams ask for more of it: more scenarios, more drafts, faster turnarounds. Some of the freed time is absorbed that way. How much is genuinely unknown. No study gives a firm-level absorbed share; the bench's 25 percent, with a range of 0 to 50, is an assumption and is labelled as one.

The best evidence on what happens instead is Humlum and Vestergaard's Danish study, which links adoption surveys to administrative records. It finds precise null effects on earnings and recorded hours, ruling out effects larger than 2 percent two years after ChatGPT's launch, and finds employers absorbing AI through reorganisation, including new tasks in content generation, AI oversight and AI integration. Other studies read the job picture differently, and that disagreement is real; the labour economics of it is taught in Economics, session 7 ↗.

Self-studyWhat happens if the cut is made on the paper hours2 min▶

Suppose Marlowe cut 106 analysts on the strength of the 106.6 freed FTE, before review and demand. The bench prints the result: a shortfall of 79.9 FTE, 71.8 of it review and 8.7 absorbed demand, and because exposed tasks skew to entry-level work in the bench's stated model, the junior grade falls from 405 to 299. Those people are the firm's future seniors. Session 3 takes up the junior rung, and session 4 lets you run the cut yourself.

The wider lesson: the four destinations are not a sequence you can skip. Review and demand claim their share whether or not the plan budgets for them. A plan that ignores them does not avoid the cost; it moves it onto the people left in the role.

Real world

Adoption shows up as reorganisation, not yet as earnings. Humlum and Vestergaard (NBER WP 33777, revised March 2026) find, in Denmark, precise nulls on earnings and recorded hours two years after ChatGPT's launch, ruling out effects larger than 2 percent, while employers reorganise tasks and create new ones in content generation, AI oversight and AI integration. Read at firm level, that is the "slack, or new tasks" box filling up before the "smaller team" box does. How adoption is led inside the firm is taught in AI leadership ↗.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Marlowe's analysts are 81.8 percent exposed by task count, but the bench frees 11.8 percent of their time before review. What explains most of the drop?

The step from 81.8 to 11.8 percent is rung 1 to rung 2, before review or demand enter. It comes from weighting tasks by time and saving only part of each exposed task's time (25 percent for E1, 12.5 percent for E2, both assumptions). B and C are real, but they come after; A is a different question about raters.

2 · Support agents gained 14 percent, novices 34 percent, consultants were about 25 percent faster, and experienced developers were 19 percent slower. What should the board paper do with these numbers?

The studies measure different things (throughput, time, correctness) on different workers, so an average means nothing. The disagreement is live and the honest move is a range with its conditions, then the firm's own data. Dropping the inconvenient result is choosing a side, not resolving the question.

3 · Time shares and speed-up free 106.6 FTE of analysts on paper. Which plan follows from the evidence?

Review and demand claim their share whether or not the plan budgets for them; on the defaults they take 80.5 of the 106.6 FTE, and a cut on the paper hours leaves a 79.9 FTE shortfall. The remaining 26.1 is a leadership choice. D confuses the macro evidence, which shows reorganisation before earnings effects, with a reason not to plan.
